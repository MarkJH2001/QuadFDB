Real flights reveal the fault response and the controller’s compensation.

A benchmark built around real flight

Feedback control can keep a quadrotor close to hover even when one motor loses actuation. If the fault lasts only a few hundred milliseconds, a diagnostic method must recognize a subtle response and identify the motor before the event ends.

QuadFDI brings together a dataset of 1,680 real flights, a fixed evaluation protocol, and baseline results. The study also introduces Residual-Augmented Gradient Boosting (RAGB), which combines healthy motion prediction errors, local motor class probabilities, and observed flight histories.

Real flight dataset

Controlled faults. Recorded feedback.

Three Crazyflie 2.1+ quadrotors perform the same hover task with the standard PID controller. An onboard injector scales one motor’s PWM command after power distribution, while the feedback loop remains active. The records preserve the commanded actuation, resulting motion, and event timing.

156 fault conditions × 10 repetitions give 1,560 fault flights, complemented by 120 healthy controls. Four motor identities, three temporal profiles, and thirteen tested multipliers cover mild through severe faults.

Event evaluation

The first usable alarm matters.

A high window classification score does not establish that a brief fault was diagnosed in time. QuadFDI evaluates whether a fresh alarm occurs during the event and whether the first alarm identifies the correct motor.

Six repetitions per condition are used for fitting, two for calibration, and two for testing. Every window from a flight stays in the same partition. Mild faults form one task; moderate and severe faults share a second fitted model and threshold.

Diagnostic method

Residual-Augmented Gradient Boosting

RAGB compares measured motion with a healthy prediction conditioned on past states and motor commands. Prediction residuals describe departures from healthy behavior; a local random forest supplies class probabilities for healthy flight and each motor fault.

The healthy predictor is fitted to healthy windows. The local RF learns motor attribution from labelled flight windows. Three-fold cross-fitting by flight produces the training features for the boosted classifier, so the final model learns from held-out predictor and RF outputs.

The two additions serve different purposes: residuals make changes in the command–motion relationship explicit, while class posteriors summarize local attribution. Controlled comparisons examine how much each contributes under both boosting and a neural classifier.

Benchmark results

Compare diagnosis, false alarms, and delay.

Nine tree, kernel, and neural references are evaluated alongside RAGB using the same flight partitions, available observations, and alarm rule. The strongest method depends on the fault regime and the diagnostic objective.

For mild faults, RAGB reaches 84.3% mean joint isolation, compared with 77.8% for RF. This gain accompanies more healthy alarms: 3.31 versus 2.17 per minute. Moderate and severe responses are easier to identify, bringing differences in false alarms and event coverage into focus.

Residuals help both classifiers on mild faults.

Adding residuals raises mild-fault joint isolation from 62.5% to 77.8% for HGB and from 53.2% to 65.7% for the control MLP. RF posteriors have a larger effect under boosting than under the neural classifier.

Including every input does not improve every regime. For severe faults, HGB with residuals reaches 99.5% joint isolation, compared with 98.8% for the full inputs. For moderate faults, adding posteriors without residuals matches RAGB’s isolation with fewer healthy alarms.

Timing also depends on which events are detected. RAGB’s shorter conditional median delay does not imply earlier alarms on the same flights: the paired median timing difference is zero for commonly detected mild-fault flights when compared with RF.
